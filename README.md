# EU Electricity Prices & the Renewables Transition

Does a higher renewable share mean cheaper electricity, or just more volatile prices?

This project takes EU day-ahead wholesale electricity prices since 2022 and checks them
against each country's renewable generation share. DuckDB and pandas for the pipeline,
a Streamlit dashboard on top, and a short-term price forecast at the end.

Work in progress. Dashboard and forecast still to come.

## What the data says so far

Across 29 countries, 2022 to 2026:

**Higher renewable share goes with lower prices.** The correlation between a country's
renewable share and its average wholesale price is -0.42. Norway sits at 98.7% renewable
and 70 EUR/MWh. Czechia is at 15.3% and 132 EUR/MWh.

**It also goes with bigger swings.** Renewable share against price volatility runs +0.31.
Volatility here is the coefficient of variation, so countries at different price levels
can be compared fairly. Norway and Sweden are the two cheapest countries in the set and
also among the most volatile.

So the answer is both, not either. Renewables look like they buy you a lower average price
and pay for it in instability.

## Data

Wholesale prices come from Ember's European wholesale electricity price data: monthly
averages, 32 countries, 2015 to 2026.

Renewable share comes from Our World in Data, share of electricity generated by renewables.

The two join on ISO3 code rather than country name, which avoids the usual spelling
mismatches entirely.

## Caveats

These are day-ahead wholesale prices, what generators get paid on the spot market. They are
not retail prices. A household bill adds taxes, network charges and levies on top, so the
two move quite differently.

Germany and Luxembourg share a bidding zone and report identical prices in all 142 months
where they overlap, so Luxembourg is not an independent observation. The correlations above
were rechecked without it and come out slightly stronger, not weaker.

Countries entered the price dataset at different times, ranging from eleven years of
coverage down to four months. Any query that ranks countries requires a minimum number of
complete periods, otherwise a country with a handful of recent expensive months lands at
the top of the table for no real reason.

Monthly averages hide intraday price spikes, which is where a good deal of the renewables
story actually lives. Hourly data exists and would be the obvious next version.

29 countries is a small sample, and none of this is causal. Countries with lots of hydro
and wind differ from coal-heavy ones in plenty of ways besides their renewable share.

## Running it

```
python -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
pip install pandas duckdb streamlit plotly statsmodels requests
python src/load_duckdb.py
```

The CSVs in `data/raw/` are not committed. Download links are on Ember's data catalogue
and the Our World in Data grapher page.

## Stack

Python, DuckDB, pandas, Streamlit, plotly, statsmodels.
